== SEGMENT 9: CHAPTER FIVE — THE CULTURE OF POWER ==

Chapter Five addresses the most tragic issue: war. The Pope observes that the digital revolution is changing the nature of conflict. Alongside conventional warfare, there are hybrid forms such as cyberattacks, information manipulation, campaigns of influence, and the automation of strategic decisions. AI acts as an accelerating factor in these processes.

He declares that the "just war" theory, which has all too often been used to justify any kind of war, is now outdated. Humanity possesses far more effective and capable tools for promoting human life and resolving conflicts — dialogue, diplomacy, and forgiveness.

The Pope describes a troubling paradigm shift in public discourse and in decisions regarding rearmament. War is no longer treated as a last resort subject to strict ethical limits. Regional conflicts drag on, tensions escalate, and reciprocal threats become almost commonplace. Public opinion is gradually shaped by polarizing media narratives, often amplified by algorithms that prioritize conflict and confrontation.

He warns about the loss of historical memory as first-hand accounts of the Holocaust and the two World Wars are disappearing. Without a living memory of the horrors of war, political decisions risk being made on the basis of power alone.

The Pope denounces the growth of the military-industrial complex, the enormous economic interests behind war, and the arms market that thrives on conflicts. He critiques nuclear deterrence and the new arms race, noting that the evolution of nuclear arsenals — including the prospect of their tactical use — makes the use of such weapons seem less improbable.

He then addresses weapons and artificial intelligence directly. He insists that no algorithm can make war morally acceptable. AI does not remove the intrinsic inhumanity of conflict — indeed, it can only bring about conflict more quickly and render it more impersonal, lowering the threshold for resorting to violence.

The Pope establishes three non-negotiable criteria for the use of AI in warfare. First, personal responsibility: the chain of responsibility must be identifiable and verifiable — those who design, train, authorize, and employ technology must be held accountable. Second, the moral timeframe: speed and efficiency should never be the supreme motivating force for irreversible decisions made in the context of war. Third, the identification and protection of civilians: any technology that facilitates attacks without seeing the face of human beings lowers the moral threshold of conflict.

He insists that the decision to use lethal force cannot be delegated to opaque or automated processes but must remain under effective, self-aware, and responsible human control.

The Pope also addresses the crisis of multilateralism. The institutions established to safeguard a common future for all peoples appear to have been weakened. What has re-emerged is the temptation to forge a collective identity in opposition to an enemy, fueled by narratives in which each party portrays itself as a victim entitled to retribution. The force of international law is replaced by the claim that "might makes right."

He warns against a false realism that treats war as inevitable. "What is truly irresponsible," he says, "is Realpolitik — the form of political realism that sows in consciences and in society an attitude of resignation to the inevitability of war, and dismisses peace and dialogue as utopian or irrational positions."
