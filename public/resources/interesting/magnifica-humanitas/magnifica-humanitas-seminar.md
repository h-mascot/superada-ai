# Magnifica Humanitas
## A seminar on Pope Leo XIV's first social encyclical
### Approximately 18 to 20 minutes

---

## Opening: Two construction sites

Two construction sites. That is where Pope Leo XIV begins his first social encyclical, *Magnifica Humanitas*, signed at Saint Peter's on May 15, 2026, the 135th anniversary of Pope Leo XIII's *Rerum Novarum*.

The first construction site is Babel. A people gathered on a plain in Shinar, with one language, one technology, one direction. They mean to build a tower with its top in the heavens, to make a name for themselves, to never be scattered. Their project is impressive. It is also a project without God, supported by a uniformity that flattens difference and chooses homogenization over communion. The tower ends in confusion and dispersion.

The second construction site is Jerusalem under Nehemiah. The walls have collapsed. The gates have been burned. Nehemiah does not impose solutions from above. He fasts. He prays. He intercedes for the people. He examines the ruins in silence. Then he convenes the families, assigns each of them a section of the wall to rebuild, listens to their concerns, addresses opposition, and coordinates the work. The city is rebuilt by the shared responsibility of all: men, women, priests, artisans, heads of households, and young people. The walls go up because the relationships are rebuilt first.

The Holy Father asks us a question. Which construction site are we working on?

This is a 245-paragraph document, and that question runs through every page of it. The subtitle is *On Safeguarding the Human Person in the Time of Artificial Intelligence*. Our task tonight is to walk through it from start to finish.

---

## The pope and the moment

A word of context.

Pope Leo XIV took his name in conscious dedication to Leo XIII, the pope who, 135 years ago this month, published *Rerum Novarum* and gave the Church her modern social doctrine. *Rerum Novarum* addressed the *new things* of the industrial revolution. The conflict between capital and labor. The worker treated as a cost of production rather than as a person made in the image of God.

Leo XIV writes that we cannot limit ourselves to repeating his predecessor's teachings. We must ask God for the wisdom to interpret the great trends of our own time, particularly the rapid spread of digitalization, AI, and robotics. He quotes *Laudato Si'*: "Never has humanity had such power over itself." That sentence is the spark of the whole document.

One further note. The popes of the past two centuries have addressed the social question in the shadow of the State as the main driver of innovation. Leo XIV observes that the main drivers of development today are not states at all. They are private, often transnational actors with resources that surpass many governments. Technological power has taken on a predominantly private character, which makes it harder to discern, harder to govern, and harder to direct toward the common good. That is the situation he writes into.

---

## The introduction: four ways to build well

Leo gives us four principles for the good construction site.

First, build on a firm relationship with God. He quotes Augustine: "You have made us for yourself, O Lord, and our heart is restless until it rests in you."

Second, accept human limits. He warns against a technology that promises to free us from all weakness. True fulfilment is not achieved by eliminating weakness but through harmonious growth.

Third, take up shared responsibility. No one bears these challenges alone, and no one is so weak that they cannot play their part. Scientists, entrepreneurs, workers, educators, legislators, civil society, popular movements, faith communities. All are given their section of the wall.

Fourth, speak an evangelical language. Avoid humiliating or antagonistic words. Refuse both naïve enthusiasm and unfounded fear. Establish standards of discernment, then translate them into practice.

Then comes the title phrase. *Magnifica humanitas*. The grandeur of humanity. In the age of artificial intelligence, when human dignity is threatened by new forms of dehumanization, Leo writes, ours is the pressing duty to remain profoundly human. "True progress always stems from a heart open to others, an intelligence willing to listen, and a will that seeks what unites rather than what separates."

That is the prologue. The encyclical has five chapters and a conclusion. We take them in order.

---

## Chapter One: the tradition he stands in

Chapter One is a brief survey of Catholic social doctrine from Leo XIII through Francis, with stops at Pius XI, Pius XII, John XXIII, the Second Vatican Council, Paul VI, John Paul II, and Benedict XVI. The point is not historical. The point is that the Church's social doctrine is a living tradition. It is not, in Leo's words, "a project devised at a desk." Each pope has interpreted the *res novae* of his own time in the light of the Gospel. The unchanging core has remained constant. What has changed is the situation each generation has had to address. Leo's position is plain. Artificial intelligence is the *res novae* of our own time, and his job is to do for AI what Leo XIII did for industrial labor.

---

## Chapter Two: the principles

Chapter Two lays out the foundations of the Church's social doctrine, with an eye toward the AI question.

The human person is created in the image of the Triune God, for relationship. Dignity is not acquired or earned. It precedes every person, in Leo's words, "a gift that precedes and transcends each person, endowed by God as an expression of his unfailing love." He cites *Dignitas Infinita*: every human person possesses an infinite dignity, grounded in his or her very being, prevailing in and beyond every circumstance.

He is direct about an ideology he finds insidious. The suggestion that every person must earn or justify his or her own worth, attributing greater value to those who are more efficient or effective. The value of persons does not depend on what they achieve or produce. Hold on to that sentence. It returns when he turns to AI.

Then six principles.

The *common good*, which Leo describes as the social expression of the dignity recognized in every person. It is not the sum of individual benefits. It is, in his phrase, a "plus," the result of interaction and mutual influence.

The *universal destination of goods*, which Leo extends explicitly to immaterial and cultural goods. Patents. Algorithms. Digital platforms. Technological infrastructure. Data. When these are concentrated in the hands of a few, without sharing or access, a new imbalance is created that contradicts the universal destination.

*Subsidiarity*, the principle that higher authorities should not supplant what individuals, families, local communities, and intermediary organizations can do themselves. Leo applies it sharply to the digital revolution. "The highest level is not the State, but rather major economic and technological actors that exercise *de facto* power over the conditions of everyday life." Subsidiarity requires that these processes not be imposed opaquely and unilaterally.

*Solidarity*, both principle and virtue. Leo extends it to the digital ecosystem, which, like the natural environment, can be preserved or exploited, shared or monopolized.

*Social justice*, beginning with the least. The poor, migrants, refugees, the unemployed, those penalized by opaque algorithms that perpetuate prejudice and discrimination.

*Integral human development*. Growth that touches every dimension of the person, spiritual, cultural, moral, and relational, while respecting our common home and future generations.

Leo turns the principles into a question. Do AI and the digital revolution truly help individuals and peoples become more humane and more fraternal, while respecting our common home and future generations? That is the criterion. The rest of the encyclical is the answer.

He closes the chapter with what he calls an *examen* for the Church herself. Social doctrine is also an examination of conscience for the Church, who must apply these principles within her own structures first.

---

## Chapter Three: technology and dominance

Chapter Three is the heart of the document. Its title: *Technology and Dominance. The Grandeur of Humanity in Light of the Promises of AI*.

Leo opens by reviving Francis's diagnosis of the technocratic paradigm. The tendency to let the logic of efficiency, control, and profit alone shape personal, social, and economic decisions. Romano Guardini, writing after the Second World War, said "Contemporary man has not been trained to use power well." Leo says that line remains true.

Then he turns to AI itself. He is careful about what he is and is not doing. He notes that any statement about AI risks becoming quickly outdated. He notes that even those who build these systems possess only a limited understanding of how they actually function. Current AI systems, in his words, are "more cultivated than built."

What follows is the most striking passage in the chapter. Leo wants us to be clear about what AI is and is not. AI systems imitate certain functions of human intelligence, and often surpass us in speed and computational capacity. But they "do not undergo experiences, do not possess a body, do not feel joy or pain, do not mature through relationships, and do not know from within what love, work, friendship, or responsibility mean. Nor do they have a moral conscience." Even when these tools are described as capable of learning, their way of doing so is not the experience of one shaped by life and grown through choices, mistakes, forgiveness, and fidelity. It is statistical adaptation based on data and feedback.

He is direct on the moral question. AI is not morally neutral. Every system embodies choices and priorities through what it measures, ignores, and optimizes. We cannot, he writes, entrust an algorithm with the power to select who is worthy or not. He quotes Francis: an algorithm has no "compassion, mercy, forgiveness, and above all, the hope that people are able to change."

His proposal for governance has teeth. Responsibility must be defined at every stage. Accountability requires that someone account for decisions, justify them, monitor them, and when necessary remedy them. He calls for the courage to insist that the ethical frameworks shaping these systems be openly discussable and subject to shared standards of social justice. "A more moral AI is not enough," he writes, "if that morality is determined by a few."

Then he uses a phrase he tells us is close to his heart. To *disarm* AI. Not to reject it. To free it from the mentality of armed competition. To discredit the assumption that technical power automatically confers the right to govern. To restore it to the plurality of human cultures and ways of life.

In the second half of the chapter, Leo addresses the transhumanist and posthumanist currents that animate much of the AI imagination today. The dream of an enhanced human being. The dream of a human-machine hybrid. He is firm. If the human being is treated as something to be perfected or surpassed, it becomes easier to accept that some lives are less useful, less desirable, less worthy. In the name of progress, "necessary sacrifices" may be justified, placing the burden on the most vulnerable in pursuit of a supposed optimization of the species.

Against this he places the Christian understanding of self-transcendence. The Christian tradition has always held that human beings are not confined by the boundaries of their own nature. We are called to surpass ourselves. But not through escape, not through contempt for our limitations. Through fulfilment in love. Through grace. Leo quotes Francis: "We become fully human when we become more than human, when we let God bring us beyond ourselves in order to attain the fullest truth of our being." That is the radical departure from the Promethean dream. What saves humanity is not enhanced self-sufficiency. It is a relationship that liberates, a communion that transforms.

The chapter ends with Augustine's two cities. Two loves have built two cities. The earthly city, the love of self even to the contempt of God. The heavenly city, the love of God even to the contempt of self. Leo writes: "The construction of Babel or the rebuilding of Jerusalem begins within each one of us."

---

## Chapter Four: truth, work, and freedom

Chapter Four turns to three concrete fields. Truth. Work. Freedom.

On *truth*, Leo identifies AI as a powerful amplifier of disinformation. The ability to manipulate content, images, and videos at scale exposes people to biased or misleading perspectives. The truth of facts has a rational dimension that requires verification and responsible argumentation. But it is also relational, built through bonds of trust and honest exchange. Both are eroded when communication is shaped by algorithms that reward conflict.

He warns about democracy in particular. Hannah Arendt observed that the ideal subjects of totalitarian regimes are not those who are ideologically convinced, but those for whom the distinction between fact and fiction, between true and false, no longer exists. Leo quotes her directly. His response is an ecology of communication. Serious journalism. Real intermediary organizations. Schools that teach the patient work of discernment. He devotes several pages to the formation of children and the risks of early, unsupervised exposure to digital devices.

On *work*, Leo continues the line that runs from *Rerum Novarum* through *Laborem Exercens*. Work is not simply an instrument. It expresses and enhances the dignity of our lives. He is realistic about the disruption. "The convergence of automation, robotics, and artificial intelligence is rapidly transforming the very structure of work," he writes, and the new ways of working are not necessarily better. His response is not nostalgia. It is to insist on social criteria for innovation. Every introduction of automation and AI should be accompanied by verifiable measures to protect employment, retraining, and the participation of workers. Continuous training must be accessible. Corporate success must be measured against the dignity of work, not only the bottom line.

On *freedom*, he writes about two threats. The first is the attention economy. Platforms designed to capture users' time and exploit human vulnerabilities. The second is social control through massive data collection. Profiling, prediction, and what he calls the architecture of visibility, where what is amplified, hidden, rewarded, and penalized ends up shaping opinion and choice. He asks for clear rules, transparency, and proportionate limits.

Then he turns to what is, in my view, the most morally piercing section of the entire encyclical. The new forms of slavery.

Nothing in the world of AI is immaterial or magical. Every seemingly immediate and flawless response is the result of a long chain of mediation. Vast networks of natural resources, energy infrastructure, and human labor. He names the workers. Those engaged in data labeling, model training, and content moderation, often involving disturbing material. Often young people, predominantly women, working under demanding conditions for minimal wages. Then the even harsher work of extracting the rare earths. Children and adolescents in dangerous conditions, their bodies scarred so the computational flow may continue uninterruptedly.

Leo makes an arresting historical comparison. The Church took eighteen centuries to articulate a clear, absolute, and universal condemnation of slavery. During those centuries, ecclesiastical institutions owned slaves. Papal bulls in the early modern period regulated and legitimized forms of subjugation. Leo names this directly. He calls it a wound in Christian memory from which we cannot consider ourselves detached. "In the name of the Church," he writes, "I sincerely ask for pardon."

His point is not to make us feel bad about the past. His point is that this is exactly the moment to look at the supply chains beneath our devices, the working conditions hidden behind our screens, and the data colonialism that extracts genetic and epidemiological information from poor countries to train models elsewhere. He asks us not to need to apologize again in a hundred years.

---

## Chapter Five: war and the civilization of love

Chapter Five turns to war. Leo says, with sober clarity, that we are watching a paradigm shift. War is being revived as an instrument of international politics, and the ethical principles that limited its use are eroding. He notes the loss of historical memory. The first-hand witnesses of the Holocaust and the two World Wars are passing from us.

He addresses the autonomous weapons question directly. The development and use of AI in warfare must be subject to the most rigorous ethical constraints. No algorithm can make war morally acceptable. AI does not remove the inhumanity of conflict. It brings about conflict more quickly and renders it more impersonal. It lowers the threshold for resorting to violence.

He rejects the proposed solution of moralizing the machines. "Sometimes there is talk of *artificial moral agents*, as if machines were able to distinguish between right and wrong with greater consistency than a human being. Yet moral judgment cannot be reduced to calculation."

What he proposes instead is what Paul VI once called the civilization of love. Leo gives us five paths.

*Disarm words*. He returns to a line from his own first address as Pope: "Let us disarm words and we will help to disarm the world."

*Build peace through justice*. Not peace as the absence of conflict. Peace as the fruit of righteousness. Augustine: "Do you therefore wish to attain peace? Then practice justice."

*Adopt the perspective of victims*. There are conflicts in which it is unjust to remain neutral. Touch the wounded flesh. Listen to their stories.

*Cultivate a healthy realism*. Avoid both the idealism that selects facts and the cynicism that confuses observation with resignation.

*Revive dialogue*. He quotes Pius XII on the eve of the Second World War. Nothing is lost with peace. Everything can be lost with war. And he repeats his own appeal from the start of his pontificate. "War is never inevitable. Weapons can and must be silenced. Our neighbors are not first our enemies, but our fellow human beings."

That is Chapter Five.

---

## Conclusion: the song of hope

The conclusion gathers everything under a Christian program. Faith. Love. Hope. Prayer.

Faith in the Word made flesh. The Incarnation is the alternative to both Babel and the transhumanist promise. God descends into our history not by adding to himself but by emptying himself. No computational system, however sophisticated, can create a heart that gives itself, or a conscience that discerns good from evil.

Love in the Eucharist, which forms us into one body. The bread on the altar is, in Augustine's words, our own sacrament. "Be then a member of the Body of Christ, that your Amen may be true."

Hope in the construction site. Four invitations, plain enough to read on a Tuesday afternoon. Remain faithful to truth. Invest in education, starting with ourselves. Cultivate relationships, especially those that require physical presence. Love justice and peace.

Leo borrows a line from Tolkien to describe what this looks like in practice. "It is not our part to master all the tides of the world, but to do what is in us for the succour of those years wherein we are set, uprooting the evil in the fields that we know, so that those who live after may have clean earth to till."

He ends with Mary. With the *Magnificat*. Mary stands before Elizabeth and sees what is invisible. God has *already* cast down the mighty. *Already* lifted up the lowly. *Already* filled the hungry. Nothing has changed in her circumstances. The Romans still occupy her land. But everything has changed in her. Leo wants the same for us.

The final word of the document belongs to her. May the Mother of Christ guide our steps through this time of change, that we may bear witness to the grandeur of humanity in which God has made his dwelling.

That is *Magnifica Humanitas*.

Thank you. I would be glad to take any questions.
