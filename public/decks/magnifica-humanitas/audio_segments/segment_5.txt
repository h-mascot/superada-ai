== SEGMENT 5: CHAPTER THREE — WHAT MUST NOT BE LOST, TRANSHUMANISM, GRACE ==

Having considered the governance of AI, the Pope now turns to the deeper question: what does it mean to safeguard our humanity? The risk extends beyond the misuse of certain technologies. More gravely, the pervasive technocratic paradigm threatens to normalize an anti-human vision — where the fullness of life is equated with having more, reducing weakness, eliminating uncertainty, and exerting total control.

The Pope insists that elevating any single dimension of human existence to an absolute is always a mistake. Intelligence, when absolutized, overshadows other essential dimensions — affection, the will, commitment, and relationships. Technical power, if left unbalanced, does not make us more capable; it makes us more isolated and more vulnerable to being dominated and excluded.

The quality of a civilization, he says, is measured not by the power of its means but by the care it is able to offer. Reading stories to a child, offering company to an elderly person, arranging a home so that it is welcoming — these simple gestures teach us to value care at a societal level.

The Pope then turns to transhumanism and posthumanism — currents of thought that interpret progress as surpassing the human condition. These perspectives form the ideological background present in some centers of technological power. The key issue is not the use of technology as such but the vision that underlies it. If the human being is treated as something to be perfected or surpassed, it becomes easier to accept that some lives are less useful, less desirable, or less worthy.

The Pope makes a clear distinction: "It is one thing to integrate technology within a human-centered, relational vision; it is quite another to be guided by an outlook that devalues human limits and promises a purely technical form of salvation."

This leads to a profound reflection on human limits. Our relationship with life seems to be in crisis today. Everything that appears as a limit — incapacity, illness, old age, suffering, vulnerability — tends to be seen primarily as a defect to be corrected. And yet humanity flourishes not despite limitations but often through them. Compassion, generosity, and spiritual experience emerge precisely within our limitations.

The Pope cites Viktor Frankl's searing insight: "We have come to know man as he really is. After all, man is that being who invented the gas chambers of Auschwitz; however, he is also that being who entered those gas chambers upright, with the Lord's Prayer or the Shema Yisrael on his lips."

He lists institutions that protect shared life — the International Committee of the Red Cross, the United Nations, the Universal Declaration of Human Rights, the Refugee Convention — and exemplary figures like Martin Luther King, Nelson Mandela, Mother Teresa, and countless others. He honors the "martyrs of everyday life" — parents, nurses, doctors, volunteers — whose testimony demonstrates that goodness does not advance automatically but requires perseverance and interior conversion.

The Pope then presents the Christian alternative to transhumanism. The expression "more than human" is not an exclusive domain of technological promise. For centuries, the Christian tradition has maintained that human beings are called to self-transcendence, not through an escape from reality but through their fulfillment in love. Citing Pope Francis: "We become fully human when we become more than human, when we let God bring us beyond ourselves in order to attain the fullest truth of our being."

What saves humanity is not enhanced self-sufficiency but a relationship that liberates, a communion that transforms. For an algorithm, an error is a flaw to be corrected; for a person, however, an error can be a catalyst for profound change. A person's future is not calculable but depends on one's freedom — elevated by the inexhaustible grace of God — and on the relationships cultivated.

The Pope closes Chapter Three with Augustine's two loves. "Two loves have built two cities: the earthly city, the love of self even to the contempt of God; the heavenly city, the love of God even to the contempt of self." The construction of Babel or the rebuilding of Jerusalem begins within each one of us.

The ultimate criterion, he says, is the question posed by Saint John Paul the Second: Does AI make human life on earth "more human" in every aspect of that life? Does it make it more worthy of man? If the answer is yes, then we can recognize it as an opportunity to be embraced responsibly. If, however, power grows while the heart withers and human bonds fray, then we are faced with a new form of Babel.
