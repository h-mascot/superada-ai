=== MAGNIFICA HUMANITAS — AUDIOBOOK SCRIPT ===
=== Pope Leo XIV — On Safeguarding the Human Person in the Time of Artificial Intelligence ===
=== Published 15 May 2026 ===

== SEGMENT 1: INTRODUCTION AND OVERVIEW ==

Welcome to this audiobook walkthrough of "Magnifica Humanitas" — "The Grandeur of Humanity" — the encyclical letter of His Holiness Pope Leo the Fourteenth, published on the fifteenth of May, 2026. This is a landmark document, addressing what may be the most urgent moral question of our time: how to safeguard the human person in the age of artificial intelligence.

The Pope opens with a powerful framing. He says, and I quote: "Humanity, created by God in all its grandeur, is today facing a pivotal choice: either to construct a new Tower of Babel or to build the city in which God and humanity dwell together."

This choice between Babel and Jerusalem — between the pride of self-sufficient power and the humility of shared rebuilding — becomes the guiding image for the entire encyclical. Every generation inherits the task of shaping its own era, but every era also runs the risk of creating an inhumane and more unjust world. And when humanity is in danger of marring its true identity, Christians lift their eyes to the Incarnate God, knowing that it is only in the mystery of the Word made flesh that the mystery of humanity truly becomes clear.

The Pope situates this document explicitly within the tradition of Catholic Social Doctrine, which began with Pope Leo the Thirteenth's encyclical "Rerum Novarum" in 1891 — the one-hundred-and-thirty-fifth anniversary of which we celebrate this year. Just as Leo the Thirteenth faced the "new things" of the industrial revolution, Pope Leo the Fourteenth faces the "new things" of the digital revolution: artificial intelligence, robotics, and the unprecedented transformation of human life by technology.

He makes a crucial point early on: technology should not be considered, in itself, as a force antagonistic to humanity. It has formed part of our history since the beginning. But today's situation is new. The power and prevalence of emerging technologies are interwoven into the fabric of daily life, shaping decision-making processes and deeply affecting the collective imagination. Never has humanity had such power over itself.

And here's a key insight: in the past, it was largely up to the State to guide and direct innovation. But today, the main drivers of development are private, often transnational parties — endowed with resources and the capacity to intervene that surpass those of many governments. Technological power thus takes on an unprecedented, predominantly private aspect.

For this reason, the Pope insists, we must begin a shared discernment process. We cannot let the succession of emergencies dictate our path. Crucial questions impose themselves on our conscience: Where are we going? Toward what goal do we wish to orient ourselves? What direction should we choose as a people and as a human community?

To answer these questions, the Pope brings forward two biblical images. The first is the Tower of Babel — a project conceived without reference to God, built on pride and the claim to self-sufficiency. The result is not unity but dispersion. The second is the Book of Nehemiah — a city rebuilt through prayer, shared responsibility, and the cooperation of all: men, women, priests, artisans, heads of households, and young people all playing their part. It is an undertaking with God at the center.

The Pope warns against what he calls the "Babel syndrome" — the idolatry of profit that sacrifices the weak, a uniformity that neutralizes differences, and the pretense that a single language — even a digital one — can translate everything, including the mystery of the person, into data and performance. Instead, he calls us to choose the "way of Nehemiah," which highlights the importance of working together to make the City of God a safe place for returning exiles.

He then outlines four conditions for authentic building. First, building for the common good requires a firm relationship with God. Second, it means accepting the limits and weakness of humanity without considering them an error to be corrected. Third, it requires shared responsibility and courage — no one can single-handedly bear the weight of the challenges the world is facing, just as no one is so weak that they cannot play their part. And fourth, it requires an evangelical language — avoiding humiliating or antagonistic words, opting rather for clarity that sheds light.

The Pope concludes his introduction with a heartfelt appeal: "Let us not be afraid to get our hands dirty on the construction site of our time. Like Nehemiah, let us pray, plan wisely, and work perseveringly, placing God at the forefront of our actions and the human person at the center of our choices."

With that, we enter the body of the encyclical.

== SEGMENT 2: CHAPTER ONE — A DYNAMIC APPROACH FAITHFUL TO THE GOSPEL ==

Chapter One presents the Church's Social Doctrine as a living, dynamic tradition — not an inert set of concepts but a living corpus of truth that safeguards and interprets humanity's vocation to a full and just life.

The Pope begins by establishing how the Church exists in history. The Church is present in the world as a sign of unity for the entire human family. She recognizes today's questions and challenges as the current setting in which to carry out her particular vocation of listening, dialogue, and service. She cannot consider herself a stranger to the forces shaping society. On the contrary, she actively participates in the processes by which society grows and is organized.

Crucially, the Pope affirms the autonomy of earthly realities — that created things and societies themselves enjoy their own laws and values. This was a key teaching of the Second Vatican Council's document "Gaudium et Spes," whose sixtieth anniversary was celebrated in December 2025. At the same time, the Church does not claim to assume the functions belonging to the State. She acts rather like the Good Samaritan — with discretion and closeness, drawing near to the wounds of humanity whenever they surface with greater severity.

The Pope then discusses the Church's engagement with human knowledge. The Church regards all who sincerely seek truth, goodness, and beauty as companions on the journey. She is not afraid to encounter human knowledge. The contributions of philosophy and the human and social sciences are essential for understanding and analyzing cultural, economic, and political dynamics. But the Pope notes that the Church does not claim to offer a definitive opinion on every specific question — she recognizes the importance of listening to scientific research and encouraging honest debate.

This leads to a beautiful image: Social Doctrine as shared discernment. The truth is not a territory to be defended but a good to be shared. The Pope cites his own earlier address, saying the Church "does not claim to possess a monopoly on truth." He invokes Pope Francis's insight that "time is greater than space" — what matters most is not occupying positions of power or defending cultural strongholds, but initiating good processes and enabling them to mature.

The truth of the Gospel is not imposed from above. It grows over time within the concrete interweaving of lives, communities, and cultures. It does not fear diversity but welcomes and guides it. The Pope illustrates this with the image of a multifaceted polyhedron, in which the one truth of the Gospel is reflected from different angles.

The remainder of Chapter One traces the development of Social Doctrine from Pope Leo the Thirteenth through Pope Francis. This is a rich summary of the Church's social teaching across more than a century. The Pope reviews the contributions of each pontiff — Leo the Thirteenth's "Rerum Novarum" defending workers' dignity, Pius the Eleventh's "Quadragesimo Anno" formulating the principle of subsidiarity, Pius the Twelfth's Christmas radio messages on international order, John the Twenty-Third's "Pacem in Terris" linking human dignity to fundamental rights, the Second Vatican Council's "Gaudium et Spes" presenting a Church close to humanity, Paul the Sixth's vision of integral human development as the new name for peace, John Paul the Second's profound reflections on work and solidarity, Benedict the Sixteenth's insistence on charity in truth at the heart of Social Doctrine, and Pope Francis's integration of ecology, fraternity, and synodality.

The Pope notes that this overview shows the Church's Social Doctrine is not the result of a project devised at a desk, but rather the product of a patient process in which each pontiff made a unique contribution in light of the "new things" of each particular era. The result is a harmonious, though not always linear, development — marked by different emphases, progressive insights, and at times changes in perspective that do not break with what came before but allow its implications to mature.

== SEGMENT 3: CHAPTER TWO — FOUNDATIONS AND PRINCIPLES ==

Chapter Two is the doctrinal heart of the encyclical. Here, the Pope articulates the foundations and principles that will guide discernment about artificial intelligence.

The foundation of everything is the mystery of the living God, revealed in Jesus Christ — a communion of Persons, Father, Son, and Holy Spirit — who is love itself in relationship. The human person is created in the image and likeness of this Triune God. Human dignity does not depend on a person's abilities, wealth, or position in life. It is a gift that precedes and transcends each person, endowed by God as an expression of his unfailing love.

The Pope makes an important distinction regarding dignity. He identifies four senses: moral dignity, which concerns how a person directs his or her choices; social dignity, which refers to living conditions and respect from society; existential dignity, meaning how a person perceives his or her own worth; and most importantly, ontological dignity — the dignity that belongs to every human being simply by virtue of existing, of having been willed, created, and loved by God. No sin, failure, humiliation, or exclusion can diminish this profound value.

He cites the recent declaration "Dignitas Infinita," which states that every human person possesses an infinite dignity, inalienably grounded in his or her very being. This dignity is infinite for two reasons: first, because the love of God who calls us to friendship with him is infinite; and second, because his love is absolutely unconditional — even if we search endlessly, we will never find anything that can erase or deny it.

The Pope then affirms the supreme value of human rights, gratefully acknowledging the Universal Declaration of Human Rights as one of the highest expressions of the human conscience of our time. He warns, however, of two serious dangers: first, that these rights are declared in a purely formal sense while violations continue; and second, the inability to recognize the foundation of their universality — the abandonment of the search for solid foundations sustaining our decisions and our laws.

He specifically calls out the gap in women's rights worldwide, noting that "doubly poor are those women who endure situations of exclusion, mistreatment, and violence, since they are frequently less able to defend their rights." It is not enough to state that men and women have equal dignity — it must be reflected in concrete decisions.

From these foundations, the Pope turns to the principles of Social Doctrine. The first is the common good — the sum total of social conditions which allow people to reach their fulfillment more fully and more easily. The common good is not the sum of individual interests. The whole is greater than the sum of its parts. It is the result of interdependence that creates a network of social good. At the international level, the Pope insists that no attempt to eliminate or subjugate a nation is gravely immoral and therefore unacceptable.

The second principle is the universal destination of goods. The earth's goods are given by God to the entire human family. Private property is legitimate but never absolute or inviolable. And here the Pope extends this principle in a crucial way for our time: among the goods that are universally intended for everyone, we must now include patents, algorithms, digital platforms, technological infrastructure, and data. When these goods remain concentrated in the hands of a few, a new imbalance is created that contradicts the universal destination of goods.

The third principle is subsidiarity. The role of individuals, families, local communities, and intermediary organizations should not be supplanted by higher-level authorities. Applied to the digital age, the Pope notes that the highest level is not the State but major economic and technological actors — companies and platforms that define conditions for access, rules of visibility, forms of interaction, and even economic opportunities. Subsidiarity requires that such processes not be imposed from above in an opaque and unilateral manner but be directed toward the common good with transparency, accountability, and meaningful forms of participation.

The fourth principle is solidarity — the concrete recognition that the future of each individual is connected to the future of all. No one is saved alone. The Pope emphasizes the intimate link between subsidiarity and solidarity: when subsidiarity is not linked to solidarity, it ends up becoming merely the protection of particular interests; when solidarity is not supported by subsidiarity, it degenerates into a form of welfare that does not foster responsibility.

The fifth principle is social justice, which begins with the least among us. The Pope identifies "structures of sin" — economic and cultural systems that produce inequality almost automatically. In the digital age, a just social order guarantees everyone equal access to opportunities, protects the weakest members of society, combats hate and misinformation, and subjects the use of data and technology to public oversight.

The Pope then turns to integral human development — a process in which the growth of individuals and peoples encompasses all dimensions of existence. He calls for metrics beyond GDP to measure true progress.

Finally, and perhaps most strikingly, the Pope offers an examen for the Church herself. The principles of Social Doctrine must be applied within the Church's own structures. He calls for synodality, transparency, accountability, subsidiarity in governance, solidarity nourished by the Eucharist, and justice within the Church — including listening to victims of abuse and providing just reparation. "Only to the extent that we are open to the action of the Holy Spirit," he says, "will these principles of Social Doctrine become incarnate in ecclesial life."

== SEGMENT 4: CHAPTER THREE — THE TECHNOCRATIC PARADIGM AND ARTIFICIAL INTELLIGENCE ==

Chapter Three is where the encyclical directly confronts artificial intelligence. The Pope returns to the building metaphor: the Tower of Babel, where collective effort follows a plan that dominates and dehumanizes, and the ruins of Jerusalem, rebuilt piece by piece as a project of shared responsibility.

He begins by denouncing the technocratic paradigm — the tendency to let the logic of efficiency, control, and profit alone shape personal, social, and economic decisions. When technology becomes the standard by which everything is judged, it begins to dictate what matters and what can be discarded, reducing creation to an object of exploitation and human beings to mere cogs in a system driven toward ever greater efficiency.

The Pope cites Romano Guardini's prescient warning: "Contemporary man has not been trained to use power well." Technological progress without ethical progress results in "having more" without "being more."

He then offers a sober, theologically informed definition of artificial intelligence. He insists that we must avoid equating this type of intelligence with that of human beings. These systems merely imitate certain functions of human intelligence. They do not undergo experiences, do not possess a body, do not feel joy or pain, do not mature through relationships, and do not know from within what love, work, friendship, or responsibility mean. They may imitate language, behavior, and analytical skills, or even simulate empathy, but they do not understand what they produce.

The Pope identifies three particular risks in personal use of AI. First, the ease with which results are obtained can weaken personal creativity and judgment. Second, the apparent objectivity of AI responses can lead us to overlook the cultural assumptions embedded in the systems. And third — perhaps most subtly — the artificial imitation of human communication can be deeply misleading. When words are simulated, they do not build genuine relationships but only their appearance. The danger is not that a person may believe they are communicating with another person, but that they may gradually lose the very desire to form genuine human connections.

At the societal level, the Pope highlights AI's environmental impact — requiring enormous amounts of energy and water, significantly influencing carbon dioxide emissions. He calls for sustainable technological solutions.

The Pope then turns to responsibility and governance. He makes a crucial claim: AI is never morally neutral. Every technical tool embodies choices and priorities through what it measures, ignores, and optimizes, and how it classifies people and situations. Ethical discernment cannot be limited to asking whether we are using a system for good or bad purposes — it must also examine how that system is designed and what vision of the human person and society is embedded in the data and models that guide it.

He calls for accountability at every stage — from those who design and develop these systems to those who use them and rely on them for concrete decisions. He warns that the internal processes leading to a result often remain opaque, making it harder to assign responsibility and correct errors.

Here the Pope introduces a striking demand: "A more moral AI is not enough if that morality is determined by a few. What is needed is a more active political involvement that is capable of slowing things down when everything is accelerating."

He insists that ownership of data cannot be left solely in private hands but must be appropriately regulated. Data is the product of many contributors and should not be treated as something to be sold off or entrusted to a select few. He calls for creative thinking to manage data as a common or shared good.

And then he introduces the powerful image of "disarming" AI. Disarming AI means freeing it from the mentality of "armed" competition — which today is not limited simply to the military context but is also an economic and cognitive phenomenon. It means discrediting the assumption that technical power automatically confers the right to govern. To disarm does not mean rejecting technology, but preventing it from dominating humanity. It means freeing technology from monopolistic control and restoring it to the plurality of human cultures and ways of life.

The Pope addresses a special appeal to AI developers: technological innovation can represent human participation in the divine act of creation. Developers therefore bear a particular ethical and spiritual responsibility, for every design choice reflects a vision of humanity.

== SEGMENT 5: CHAPTER THREE — WHAT MUST NOT BE LOST, TRANSHUMANISM, GRACE ==

Having considered the governance of AI, the Pope now turns to the deeper question: what does it mean to safeguard our humanity? The risk extends beyond the misuse of certain technologies. More gravely, the pervasive technocratic paradigm threatens to normalize an anti-human vision — where the fullness of life is equated with having more, reducing weakness, eliminating uncertainty, and exerting total control.

The Pope insists that elevating any single dimension of human existence to an absolute is always a mistake. Intelligence, when absolutized, overshadows other essential dimensions — affection, the will, commitment, and relationships. Technical power, if left unbalanced, does not make us more capable; it makes us more isolated and more vulnerable to being dominated and excluded.

The quality of a civilization, he says, is measured not by the power of its means but by the care it is able to offer. Reading stories to a child, offering company to an elderly person, arranging a home so that it is welcoming — these simple gestures teach us to value care at a societal level.

The Pope then turns to transhumanism and posthumanism — currents of thought that interpret progress as surpassing the human condition. These perspectives form the ideological background present in some centers of technological power. The key issue is not the use of technology as such but the vision that underlies it. If the human being is treated as something to be perfected or surpassed, it becomes easier to accept that some lives are less useful, less desirable, or less worthy.

The Pope makes a clear distinction: "It is one thing to integrate technology within a human-centered, relational vision; it is quite another to be guided by an outlook that devalues human limits and promises a purely technical form of salvation."

This leads to a profound reflection on human limits. Our relationship with life seems to be in crisis today. Everything that appears as a limit — incapacity, illness, old age, suffering, vulnerability — tends to be seen primarily as a defect to be corrected. And yet humanity flourishes not despite limitations but often through them. Compassion, generosity, and spiritual experience emerge precisely within our limitations.

The Pope cites Viktor Frankl's searing insight: "We have come to know man as he really is. After all, man is that being who invented the gas chambers of Auschwitz; however, he is also that being who entered those gas chambers upright, with the Lord's Prayer or the Shema Yisrael on his lips."

He lists institutions that protect shared life — the International Committee of the Red Cross, the United Nations, the Universal Declaration of Human Rights, the Refugee Convention — and exemplary figures like Martin Luther King, Nelson Mandela, Mother Teresa, and countless others. He honors the "martyrs of everyday life" — parents, nurses, doctors, volunteers — whose testimony demonstrates that goodness does not advance automatically but requires perseverance and interior conversion.

The Pope then presents the Christian alternative to transhumanism. The expression "more than human" is not an exclusive domain of technological promise. For centuries, the Christian tradition has maintained that human beings are called to self-transcendence, not through an escape from reality but through their fulfillment in love. Citing Pope Francis: "We become fully human when we become more than human, when we let God bring us beyond ourselves in order to attain the fullest truth of our being."

What saves humanity is not enhanced self-sufficiency but a relationship that liberates, a communion that transforms. For an algorithm, an error is a flaw to be corrected; for a person, however, an error can be a catalyst for profound change. A person's future is not calculable but depends on one's freedom — elevated by the inexhaustible grace of God — and on the relationships cultivated.

The Pope closes Chapter Three with Augustine's two loves. "Two loves have built two cities: the earthly city, the love of self even to the contempt of God; the heavenly city, the love of God even to the contempt of self." The construction of Babel or the rebuilding of Jerusalem begins within each one of us.

The ultimate criterion, he says, is the question posed by Saint John Paul the Second: Does AI make human life on earth "more human" in every aspect of that life? Does it make it more worthy of man? If the answer is yes, then we can recognize it as an opportunity to be embraced responsibly. If, however, power grows while the heart withers and human bonds fray, then we are faced with a new form of Babel.

== SEGMENT 6: CHAPTER FOUR — TRUTH AS A COMMON GOOD ==

Chapter Four applies the principles to three concrete domains: truth and communication, work, and freedom. The Pope begins with truth as a common good.

He observes that digital platforms and AI systems are driving profound changes in public and political communication. Tools that could foster dialogue and participation are often used to construct distorted narratives and blur the boundaries between truth and falsehood. Disinformation did not begin with AI, yet today it finds a powerful amplifier in AI.

The Pope warns that those who command powerful technological and economic resources possess significant capabilities for influencing cultural change — ultimately, they can influence a significant number of people concerning the truth about humanity, the world, the meaning of existence, the family, and even God. This is pure power detached from truth.

At its root lies a deeper sickness: modern man is wrongly convinced that he is the sole author of himself, his life, and society. Once the idea of a universal truth about the good, knowable by human reason, is lost, inevitably the notion of conscience also changes. The Pope cites Pope Francis: "What is law without the conviction, born of age-old reflection and great wisdom, that each human being is sacred and inviolable?"

The search for truth is an essential element of democracy. When questions about what is true lose their appeal, and a pragmatism takes hold that is content with what appears useful or effective, democratic life is weakened. Indifference to truth leads, slowly but surely, to a descent into totalitarianism. The Pope cites Hannah Arendt's chilling observation: the ideal subjects of such regimes are those for whom the distinction between fact and fiction — the reality of experience — and the distinction between true and false — the standards of thought — no longer exist.

The Pope calls for an ecology of communication. On the level of public policy, this means establishing norms so that decision-making behind content selection becomes more transparent and protects personal data. For families and schools, there is a growing need for formation concerning the proper and critical use of digital tools. He acknowledges with shame that the Church herself has not always been transparent, thanking journalists who have brought abuses to light.

He then turns to education, which he calls decisive in an era when truth is often distorted. The pervasiveness of digital media fosters a culture of immediacy and hyper-stimulation, giving rise to fatigue, boredom, and apathy concerning the effort required for seeking the truth. Education, by contrast, is a long journey requiring patience.

The Pope warns about early and unsupervised exposure to digital devices — negative impacts on sleep, attention span, control of emotions, and relationships, especially during the most vulnerable stages of life. He calls for a legislative alliance to protect minors: age limits, holding service providers accountable, and providing specific protections against all forms of online sexual exploitation and violence.

Schools have a central role but face three major challenges: socio-political inequality in access to education, pedagogical challenges as curricula become obsolete, and intellectual challenges as knowledge becomes increasingly fragmented. The Pope insists that schools are not called to follow the pace of the digital world but to offer that which the digital sphere by itself cannot provide — a shared time for learning and developing trustworthy relationships.

== SEGMENT 7: CHAPTER FOUR — THE DIGNITY OF WORK ==

The Pope now turns to work, which since the emergence of the Church's Social Doctrine has been recognized as the essential key to understanding the entire social question. Work is not simply an instrument — it expresses and enhances the dignity of our lives. It is a requirement of the human condition, a normal path toward maturity, development, and personal fulfillment.

Today, the convergence of automation, robotics, and AI is rapidly transforming the very structure of work. The Pope warns that while AI promises to boost productivity by taking over mundane tasks, it frequently forces workers to adapt to the speed and demands of machines rather than machines being designed to support those who work. Current approaches to technology can paradoxically de-skill workers, subject them to automated surveillance, and relegate them to rigid and repetitive tasks.

The Pope recognizes unemployment as a grave evil. Today, amid the fourth industrial revolution, this concern is even more acute as innovation is often pursued solely for reducing costs and increasing profits. In some contexts, there is a legitimate fear of a significant and rapid contraction in available jobs that would create a chain reaction deeply impacting families, young people, and local economies.

He insists that the protection of employment opportunities and the irreplaceable role of the individual must remain the general rule. The pursuit of greater profits cannot justify choices that systematically sacrifice jobs, because the human person is an end, not a means.

The Pope proposes three concrete paths forward. First, every introduction of automation and AI should be accompanied by verifiable measures to protect the employment, retraining, and participation of workers. Second, proactive policies are needed that make continuous training and professional transitions accessible to all, ensuring that the cost of adaptation does not fall solely on individuals. Third, there needs to be a corporate commitment to include quality and dignity of work among its indicators of success.

He makes a powerful statement: "More than ever, in the age of AI and robotics, it is no longer possible to rely solely on the invisible hand of the market. Politics has the task of orientating economies and technologies to the common good, promoting dignified work, social inclusion, and an equitable distribution of the benefits of innovation."

The Pope calls for metrics beyond Gross Domestic Product to measure development, noting that GDP almost systematically neglects aspects essential to the overall wellbeing of people and the environment. He critiques finance that operates without anthropological and moral foundations, and warns that income from capital risks replacing income from labor.

He emphasizes that the family is a primary social good, founded on the enduring union between a man and a woman, and that when political projects relegate the family to a marginal or secondary role, the authentic growth of the entire social body is compromised. The family is a fragile social good immediately affected by economic and technological transformations, and it requires cultural, juridical, and economic support.

For young people, job insecurity is particularly devastating. Work is not merely a source of income but a crucial sphere in which identity is formed, friendships are forged, practical responsibilities are learned, and one's vocation is discerned.

== SEGMENT 8: CHAPTER FOUR — PROTECTING FREEDOM AND NEW FORMS OF SLAVERY ==

The Pope now addresses the impact of the digital revolution on human freedom. He warns that the subtler forms of addiction linked to the "digital attention economy" should not be underestimated. Platforms and services are often designed to capture users' time and attention, exploiting their vulnerabilities and weakening their inner freedom. When business models thrive on human weakness, the person is treated as a means rather than as an end.

A further risk, less visible but no less serious, is social control made possible by the massive collection of data and use of algorithmic systems. When every action — movements, purchases, relationships, and preferences — leaves a trace, a new form of power emerges: the power to profile, predict, and influence behavior, often without individuals being fully aware of it. If such data is used to make decisions affecting concrete opportunities, there is a risk of undermining freedom and discriminating against the most vulnerable.

The Pope warns that control is exercised not only through explicit prohibitions but also through the architecture of visibility — what is amplified or rendered invisible, what is rewarded or penalized — ultimately shaping opinions and choices, fostering conformity and self-censorship. Freedom in the digital age is not merely a matter of interiority but also a public concern.

He then delivers one of the most powerful sections of the entire encyclical: the denunciation of new forms of slavery directly linked to the digital economy. He insists that nothing in the world of AI is immaterial or magical. Every seemingly immediate and flawless response is the result of a long chain of mediation involving vast networks of natural resources, energy infrastructure, and, above all, people.

A significant part of the digital economy's functioning relies on the silent work of millions of people engaged in essential yet largely unseen activities — data labeling, model training, and content moderation, often involving disturbing material. In many cases, these workers are young people, predominantly women, working under demanding conditions for minimal wages. Added to this invisible labor is the even harsher work of extracting the resources required for the production of devices and microprocessors on which AI depends. In some regions of the world, children and adolescents work in dangerous conditions, crushing the materials from which rare earth elements are extracted.

Furthermore, criminal networks use online platforms, messaging systems, anonymous payment methods, and profiling techniques to recruit, control, and transport victims of trafficking — very often minors — reducing men and women to data to be tracked and packages to be moved around within the same digital circuits that support much of the global economy.

The Pope makes a searing judgment: "If technology promises emancipation, yet produces new forms of global subordination, it stands in contradiction to the fundamental principle of human dignity."

He also addresses new forms of colonialism that no longer dominate only bodies but appropriate data — transforming personal lives into exploitable information. Entire regions, especially those marked by structural fragility, are subjected to a new mindset of extraction: health data, epidemiological profiles, genetic maps, and demographic information have become the new "rare earths" of power.

In a remarkable act of honesty, the Pope acknowledges the Church's own historical complicity in slavery. He notes that in antiquity and the Middle Ages, many individuals and even ecclesiastical institutions had slaves. It was only in the nineteenth century that a formal, absolute, and universal condemnation of slavery was clearly articulated. He calls this a wound in Christian memory, one from which we cannot consider ourselves detached. "It is impossible not to feel deep sorrow," he says, "when contemplating the immense suffering and humiliation endured by so many." And then, in the name of the Church, he sincerely asks for pardon.

He concludes this section by calling for vigilance: "If we want to avoid the need to ask for pardon again in the future for having failed to respect the treasure of human dignity that is required by our faith, it falls to us today to denounce, clearly and firmly, trafficking in its many forms."

== SEGMENT 9: CHAPTER FIVE — THE CULTURE OF POWER ==

Chapter Five addresses the most tragic issue: war. The Pope observes that the digital revolution is changing the nature of conflict. Alongside conventional warfare, there are hybrid forms such as cyberattacks, information manipulation, campaigns of influence, and the automation of strategic decisions. AI acts as an accelerating factor in these processes.

He declares that the "just war" theory, which has all too often been used to justify any kind of war, is now outdated. Humanity possesses far more effective and capable tools for promoting human life and resolving conflicts — dialogue, diplomacy, and forgiveness.

The Pope describes a troubling paradigm shift in public discourse and in decisions regarding rearmament. War is no longer treated as a last resort subject to strict ethical limits. Regional conflicts drag on, tensions escalate, and reciprocal threats become almost commonplace. Public opinion is gradually shaped by polarizing media narratives, often amplified by algorithms that prioritize conflict and confrontation.

He warns about the loss of historical memory as first-hand accounts of the Holocaust and the two World Wars are disappearing. Without a living memory of the horrors of war, political decisions risk being made on the basis of power alone.

The Pope denounces the growth of the military-industrial complex, the enormous economic interests behind war, and the arms market that thrives on conflicts. He critiques nuclear deterrence and the new arms race, noting that the evolution of nuclear arsenals — including the prospect of their tactical use — makes the use of such weapons seem less improbable.

He then addresses weapons and artificial intelligence directly. He insists that no algorithm can make war morally acceptable. AI does not remove the intrinsic inhumanity of conflict — indeed, it can only bring about conflict more quickly and render it more impersonal, lowering the threshold for resorting to violence.

The Pope establishes three non-negotiable criteria for the use of AI in warfare. First, personal responsibility: the chain of responsibility must be identifiable and verifiable — those who design, train, authorize, and employ technology must be held accountable. Second, the moral timeframe: speed and efficiency should never be the supreme motivating force for irreversible decisions made in the context of war. Third, the identification and protection of civilians: any technology that facilitates attacks without seeing the face of human beings lowers the moral threshold of conflict.

He insists that the decision to use lethal force cannot be delegated to opaque or automated processes but must remain under effective, self-aware, and responsible human control.

The Pope also addresses the crisis of multilateralism. The institutions established to safeguard a common future for all peoples appear to have been weakened. What has re-emerged is the temptation to forge a collective identity in opposition to an enemy, fueled by narratives in which each party portrays itself as a victim entitled to retribution. The force of international law is replaced by the claim that "might makes right."

He warns against a false realism that treats war as inevitable. "What is truly irresponsible," he says, "is Realpolitik — the form of political realism that sows in consciences and in society an attitude of resignation to the inevitability of war, and dismisses peace and dialogue as utopian or irrational positions."

== SEGMENT 10: CHAPTER FIVE — BUILDING THE CIVILIZATION OF LOVE ==

Against the culture of power, the Pope proposes the civilization of love — a demanding project that consists in translating charity into structures of justice, giving institutional form to fraternity, and regarding others as allies necessary for building the common good.

He insists that Christians view history in the light of the crucified and risen Lord. We do not consider the present as a predetermined fate but an opportunity for personal and collective conversion. Goodness grows silently from the earth. Even in the darkest nights, the Lord raises up men and women who refuse to give up, who persevere in doing good, who protect the vulnerable and open pathways to reconciliation.

The Pope then proposes five practical paths for building the civilization of love.

First, disarm words. "Let us disarm words and we will help to disarm the world." Peace begins with each one of us — in the way we look at others, listen to others, and speak about others. We must reject the war of words and images, the paradigm of war.

Second, build peace through justice. True peace is born of justice. The Pope cites Saint Augustine: "Do you wish to attain peace? Then practice justice!"

Third, adopt the perspective of victims. When we witness the bombing of civilians, attacks on hospitals, schools, or vital infrastructure, and violence that affects children, we are confronted with scandals that wound humanity itself. We cannot remain neutral. Giving space to the voices of victims helps us to reject the normalization of conflict and to restore to victims the dignity of being recognized and heard.

Fourth, cultivate a healthy realism — neither political idealism nor cynicism. Authentic realism does not give up on changing the world. It seeks viable paths for making peace more than a mere word, through credible institutions, verifiable guarantees, patient negotiations, conflict prevention, and the protection of civilians.

Fifth, revive dialogue. Dialogue is the primary means of coexistence between people and nations, and it is the alternative to open conflict. The Pope quotes his own appeal from the start of his pontificate: "Let us meet, let us talk, let us negotiate! War is never inevitable. Weapons can and must be silenced, for they do not resolve problems but only increase them."

He emphasizes the decisive role of interreligious dialogue. Those who use the name of God to legitimize terrorism, violence, or war betray his true nature — to fight in the name of religion means attacking religion itself.

He calls for the necessity of diplomacy and multilateralism. International organizations, particularly the United Nations, are essential instruments for promoting a civilization of love. The Holy See supports these endeavors while recognizing the need for profound reforms.

The Pope concludes this chapter by returning to prayer. Peace, he says, comes from God — it is the peace of the risen Christ. "A peace that is unarmed and disarming, humble and persevering." He invites everyone to pray for this gift and to commit to achieving it in our relationships and in society.

== SEGMENT 11: CONCLUSION — THE WORD BECAME FLESH ==

The conclusion of the encyclical proposes a sober yet demanding program of Christian life. The Pope begins by returning to the mystery of the Incarnation — the Word who became flesh and dwelt among us.

He contrasts the promises of transhumanism, which seek an enhanced and almost disembodied humanity, with the mystery of the Son of God entering into our human condition. The living God descends into our history in order to free us from all forms of slavery. He takes upon himself our weakness and transforms it into a setting for salvation.

Citing the French theologian Pierre de Bérulle: "According to the teaching of our faith, we have and adore, in our mysteries, a God who is born in a manger, a God who lives and travels in Judea, a God who dies on the cross, a dead God who lies in the tomb. O wonder… man is God and this God-Man passes through all those stages, endures all those states and ennobles them, sanctifies them, deifies them in himself!"

What saves humanity, the Pope insists, is the divine love that descends into the most fragile point of our history and renews it from within.

He then makes a beautiful affirmation: "No computational system, however sophisticated, can create a heart that gives itself, or a conscience that discerns good from evil. Even when machines excel in efficiency, a human face that asks to be gazed upon remains the center of our history."

He speaks of the mystery of recapitulation — the certainty that the Father has decreed to bring all things back to Christ, the one Head. In this plan, nothing will be lost that is authentically human. Everything will be purified and reunited in the One, who gathers every fragment of life, every tear, and every authentically human achievement, rescuing them from nothingness and delivering them, redeemed, to the Father.

== SEGMENT 12: CONCLUSION — ONE BODY, THE CONSTRUCTION SITE, THE MAGNIFICAT ==

The Pope then proposes a Eucharistic spirituality — a spirituality of ecclesial unity in love. The Eucharist is the sacrament of unity, where the Lord gives himself and gathers the Church together. The "Amen" that we say in the liturgy shapes our entire lives. Citing Saint Augustine: "Be then a member of the Body of Christ that your Amen may be true!"

The Church, nourished by the Eucharist, is called to make visible a different paradigm — one that preserves human connections, gives a voice to the invisible, and ensures that processes are aimed at respecting people's dignity.

The Pope then issues four calls for our time. First, remain faithful to the truth — cultivate hearts that love the truth, prefer what is right despite the most appealing content, and pursue wisdom rather than immediate results. Second, invest in education — we must consider the digital world as a new continent to be evangelized, one that requires generous missionaries who are mature in the faith. Third, cultivate relationships — cherish places and times where physical presence remains crucial, such as shared meals, Christian community gatherings, time spent with the lonely, and serving the poor. Fourth, love justice and peace — every technical or economic decision should include spiritual discernment and be an opportunity for assessing whether advances in AI are promoting justice and participation or concentrating wealth and power.

The Pope returns one final time to the image of Nehemiah, who heard the cry of a devastated city, brought that pain to prayer, discerned before God, asked for help, received permission to return, organized the work, confronted resistance, and rebuilt the walls of Jerusalem brick by brick. In this era of digital transformation, we are called not to be passive spectators but men and women prepared to enter the construction sites of history — research laboratories, technology companies, schools, the media, institutions, and local communities.

And the encyclical ends with the Magnificat — Mary's song of praise and hope. The Blessed Virgin Mary teaches us to recognize God's invisible work and directs our gaze to the points at which humanity is broken and the world becomes distorted. She teaches us to look at the world from a lower position — through the eyes of those who suffer rather than the mighty, to view history through the eyes of the little ones, to interpret events from the viewpoint of the widow, the orphan, the stranger, the wounded child, the exile, and the fugitive.

The Pope entrusts our desire to the Mother of Christ, to the Woman of the Magnificat, that she may guide our steps through this time of change and preserve in each of us true faith in the Gospel, so that we may bear witness to the grandeur of humanity, in which God has made his dwelling.

And so ends "Magnifica Humanitas" — a document that calls us to abandon the construction of yet another Tower of Babel and to join forces in building up the common good, so that humanity will never lose its beauty, and the world once again will come to recognize the human heart as the place where God desires to dwell.

Given in Rome, at Saint Peter's, on the fifteenth of May, in the year 2026, the second year of the pontificate of Pope Leo the Fourteenth.

May this walkthrough serve as a guide to this important document, and may the Holy Spirit bring about the civilization of love in our lives, even in the era of artificial intelligence.
