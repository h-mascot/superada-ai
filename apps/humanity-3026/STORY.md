# Humanity 3026

*A thousand years back. A thousand years forward.*

In the year 1026, nobody alive could have imagined you. Not your phone, not your medicine, not the photograph of Earth taken from the Moon — not even the plain, ordinary fact that you will probably live past seventy.

This story tries to return the favour. It measures how far humanity travelled between 1026 and 2026, and then asks what the *same distance* would look like if we walked it again.

## I. The Thousand-Year Mirror
<!-- viz: mirror | year: 1026 | era: Prologue | accent: #e3b86b -->

Stand in 2026 and look back, and you can see a thousand years of road behind you. Most of it is flat. For century after century, a person's life looked very much like their grandparents' lives. Then, in the last two hundred years, the road tilts upward and becomes a cliff.

Now turn around. The road ahead is dark. We cannot see it — but we can measure the road behind us and hold it up like a mirror.

The method is simple, unscientific, and that is the point. Take the ways that life changed between 1026 and 2026. Ask how many *times* bigger, faster, longer or stranger each one became. Then imagine that same ratio happening all over again.

What comes out is not a prediction. It is a sense of scale. The world of 3026 should seem as impossible to us as we would seem to someone living in 1026. Anything less is not imaginative enough.

## II. A Candle in 1026
<!-- viz: candle | year: 1026 | era: The World Before | accent: #e8a55a -->

In 1026 there are perhaps three hundred million people on Earth — fewer than live in the United States today. Most of them will never travel more than a few days' walk from the place they were born.

The fastest anything moves is a galloping horse. A message crosses a kingdom in weeks. Night is dark everywhere, lit only by fire. Nearly all the energy humanity has comes from muscle, firewood, wind and falling water.

It is not a foolish world. In Cairo, Ibn al-Haytham has recently finished his *Book of Optics*, arguing that we see because light travels into the eye. In Persia, Ibn Sina has just completed *The Canon of Medicine*, which will be taught for centuries. In Sichuan, the Song government has begun issuing the world's first state paper money. A Chola fleet from southern India has crossed the Bay of Bengal to strike the empire of Srivijaya.

And five years earlier, in 1021, Norse settlers were felling trees in Newfoundland. For a brief moment, humans had closed the circle around the planet. Nobody on either side of the ocean understood what that meant.

A child born this year has roughly a coin-flip's chance of reaching adulthood. Life expectancy at birth is around thirty. Books are copied by hand, one letter at a time, by candlelight. Most people will never read one.

Hand a single page describing 2026 to the wisest scholar alive in 1026, and they would file it beside the stories of dragons.

## III. The Long Stillness, the Sudden Fire
<!-- viz: curve | year: 1026 | era: The Road Behind | accent: #f0c27a -->

For the first half of the millennium, change comes so slowly it can be mistaken for stillness. Empires rise and fall. The Black Death kills perhaps a third of Europe. The line of human population barely lifts from the floor.

Around 1040, a craftsman in China named Bi Sheng invents movable type from baked clay. Four centuries later, a goldsmith in Mainz builds a press with movable metal letters. Ideas begin to copy themselves faster than any scribe could.

Around 1800, something breaks open. Coal, and then oil, give every human the labour of dozens of invisible servants. Humanity took all of its history to reach one billion people, around 1804. It reached eight billion in 2022.

Inside a single human lifetime — 1903 to 1969 — we went from a powered flight at Kitty Hawk that lasted twelve seconds to footprints on the Moon.

> **1 → 8** · Billions of people. The first billion took all of human history. The eighth arrived in 2022.

The shape matters more than any single number. Change is not a ramp; it is compound interest. Nearly everything that would astonish a visitor from 1026 happened in the last tenth of the journey.

## IV. The Measure of a Millennium
<!-- viz: bars | year: 2026 | until: 2026 | era: The Ratio | accent: #f3d9a4 -->

Lay the two years side by side and the change stops being a feeling. It becomes a set of numbers.

> **27×** · People alive — roughly 300 million in 1026, more than 8 billion in 2026.

> **2.4×** · Life expectancy at birth — about 30 years then, about 73 now.

> **100×** · Energy humanity uses each year — from muscle and firewood to a planetary grid.

> **600×** · Fastest human travel — a galloping horse then, the Apollo capsules at nearly 40,000 km/h.

> **10,000,000×** · Speed of a message — weeks by courier, then a fraction of a second around the world.

Not every line can repeat. Earth cannot hold twenty-seven times more people. Light will not speed up because we are impatient. So the mirror has to be read with imagination. When one road hits a wall, the growth goes somewhere else — the way muscle power, once exhausted, turned into coal.

Here is the rule for the rest of this story. For each part of life, take the thousand-year ratio, apply it once more, and ask what kind of world would make that number true.

## V. Bodies Unbound
<!-- viz: helix | year: 2100 | era: The Body | accent: #9fe3d4 -->

Life expectancy multiplied by 2.4 once. Do it again and a child born in 3026 can expect to live about 175 years.

> **~175 years** · Life expectancy in 3026, if the thousand-year ratio repeats.

But that number hides the real change, just as "thirty" hid the fact that most deaths in 1026 were children. By 3026, the diseases of ageing have gone the way of smallpox. Bodies are maintained rather than repaired — continuously, quietly, before anything breaks, the way we maintain a bridge.

Most inherited diseases are corrected before birth. A few communities refuse, and their refusal is protected as fiercely as freedom of religion once was.

In 1026, a lucky person knew their grandparents. In 3026, it is ordinary to know five or six generations of your family at once — to be held as a baby by someone who remembers the twenty-ninth century.

Death does not vanish. People still die of accidents, of choice, of the simple fullness of having lived enough. But because it is rarer, grief is heavier. A funeral is a gathering of a hundred living descendants.

## VI. The Chorus
<!-- viz: network | year: 2250 | era: The Mind | accent: #8ec9ff -->

In 1026, every thought on Earth happened inside a skull. Writing was the only way to keep a thought outside one, and most people could not write.

By 2026, the world's machines perform more arithmetic every second than every human who ever lived could have done by hand in a lifetime. They are beginning to think in ways we struggle to name.

Let that ratio repeat. In 3026, most of the thinking in the solar system does not happen in skulls at all. Humans live inside a chorus of minds — some born, some built, and some that began as one and became the other.

Nobody "uses" the chorus, any more than a fish uses water. A question arrives as a feeling of not-knowing and leaves as understanding. School is no longer about memorising what is known; the chorus knows. School is about *wanting* — learning to decide what is worth knowing next.

The great political question of the age is not intelligence but consent. Who may enter your thoughts? Whose voice carries weight in the chorus? The parties of 3026 argue about the borders of the self the way the parties of 2026 argued about the borders of countries.

## VII. Holding the Sun
<!-- viz: swarm | year: 2400 | era: Energy | accent: #ffcf7a -->

Humanity's energy use grew about a hundredfold in a thousand years. Grow it a hundredfold again and you reach roughly two quadrillion watts — about one percent of all the sunlight that falls on Earth.

You cannot burn that much on a living planet. The waste heat alone would cook it. So the energy of 3026 is gathered where sunlight is endless and nothing lives: in space.

Around the Sun drifts a thin swarm of collectors — mirrors and films thinner than foil, millions of them, catching light and beaming power to wherever it is needed.

> **5 in a trillion** · The fraction of the Sun's output needed to power a civilisation using a hundred times today's energy.

It sounds colossal. It is barely a scratch. The swarm of 3026 is the first thin frost on a window the size of a star.

In 1026, energy meant a farmer with a hoe or an ox at the plough. In 3026, it is nearly free. What is scarce are the older things: time, trust, attention, beauty, and a quiet place by the water.

## VIII. Earth, Returned
<!-- viz: globe | year: 2550 | era: The Home World | accent: #8fe39a -->

In 1026, most of Earth's land was forest, grassland and wilderness. By 2026, about half of all habitable land had become farmland, and a third of humanity could no longer see the Milky Way.

In 3026, the curve has turned back. Food is grown from air, water and light in buildings a fraction of the size of the fields they replaced. The fields return to forest. Rivers find their old beds. The great herds come back to the plains.

Earth holds fewer people than it did in 2026 — a few billion — not through catastrophe, but because families shrank and many chose to live elsewhere. The home world has become something between a homeland, a garden and a pilgrimage.

People born in orbit or on Mars make the journey once in their lives, the way pilgrims once walked to Jerusalem, Mecca or Santiago — to stand in real rain, under real sky, on the ground where the story began.

Seen from above, the glowing webs of the old cities have dimmed to a scatter of warm points, like campfires. For the first time since 1026, a child almost anywhere on Earth can look up and see the Milky Way.

## IX. The Sky Becomes a Coastline
<!-- viz: orrery | year: 2700 | era: Reach | accent: #9ab8ff -->

The fastest human in 1026 rode a horse at about sixty kilometres an hour. By 2026, humans had ridden capsules at nearly forty thousand. Multiply by six hundred again and you reach twenty-four million kilometres an hour — about two percent of the speed of light.

At that speed Mars is hours away, Jupiter about a day, Neptune about a week. The solar system of 3026 is roughly the size, in travel time, that the Mediterranean was in 1026. The sky has become a coastline.

> **~220 billion** · People, if population grows twenty-seven-fold again — possible only across a whole solar system.

Most people who live beyond Earth do not live on planets. They live in rotating habitats — cylinders kilometres long, spun for gravity, with lakes and forests on the inside and the stars wheeling past beneath their feet.

Mars is a frontier, proud and harsh and argumentative. The asteroids are harbours and mines. Under the orange haze of Titan, cities glow beside lakes of liquid methane. Most traffic is slow freight on long, patient orbits, just as most medieval trade crept along the coasts.

## X. Distance Returns
<!-- viz: lightlag | year: 2800 | era: The Great Delay | accent: #7fd8ff -->

Here the mirror plays a trick. The speed of a message rose ten-million-fold in a thousand years. It cannot happen again. Light is the speed limit, and we reached it with the telegraph.

So as humanity spreads out, distance comes back. A message from Earth takes between three and twenty-two minutes to reach Mars. More than half an hour to reach Jupiter. About four hours to reach the settlements around Neptune.

> **4 hours** · One-way light delay from Earth to Neptune. A "hello" and its reply are an afternoon apart.

Real conversation across the system becomes impossible. In a strange way, the solar system of 3026 feels like the world of 1026: a patchwork of realms that hear each other's news late.

Cultures drift apart. Accents become languages. Laws diverge. The people of Neptune's moons find the people of Earth slow, sentimental and oddly obsessed with weather.

This is not a failure. In 2026, one global culture was flattening everything it touched. In 3026, the delay of light protects difference, the way oceans and mountains once did.

## XI. Many Kinds of Human
<!-- viz: tree | year: 2900 | era: Divergence | accent: #c7a6ff -->

In 1026, humanity was divided into thousands of peoples who believed they were profoundly different. In 2026, we learned the truth: any two humans share about 99.9 percent of their DNA.

For three hundred thousand years, the human family tree was a single trunk. In the third millennium, it begins to branch.

Children raised in the light gravity of Mars grow tall and fine-boned. Some habitat peoples shape their children for radiation, for cold, for the long sleep of slow journeys. Some people leave their bodies behind altogether and live entirely within the chorus. Others refuse every change and live as their ancestors did in 2026 — and are honoured for it.

"What is a human?" becomes the central question of law, faith and love. The answer the age settles on is not biological. A human is anyone who remembers being one — anyone descended, in body or in mind, from the long line of storytellers who first sat around fires in Africa.

## XII. Letters from Proxima
<!-- viz: interstellar | year: 2990 | era: The Far Shore | accent: #ff9e7a -->

In 1026, the farthest a human voice could carry was across a valley. Around November 2026, Voyager 1 becomes the first human-made object a full light-day from Earth — still whispering home, more than fifty years after launch.

The first interstellar probes, launched in the late 2700s, were small, fast, and pushed by light, travelling at a few percent of its speed. At two percent of light, the journey to Proxima Centauri takes about two hundred years.

So by 3026, humanity has been receiving letters from another star for three decades. The first images showed a world locked with one face to its dim red sun: a glare of permanent day, a night that never ends, and between them a ring of endless twilight.

> **4.24 years** · Light-delay to Proxima Centauri. Every message is an heirloom by the time its answer arrives.

Whether what the probes found along that twilight ring is life is still argued in every habitat and on every world. No human body has made the crossing. But some minds of the chorus are travelling now, sleeping in the probes — for a thousand years is a long time to wait, but not for someone who does not age.

## XIII. What the Fire Remembers
<!-- viz: embers | year: 3026 | era: What Remains | accent: #ffb070 -->

So much changes that it is worth asking what does not.

A person in 1026 sat by a fire in the evening and told stories. They loved their children, sometimes badly and sometimes well. They were afraid of the dark. They grieved the people they lost, made music, argued about the gods, and looked up at the stars and wondered what they were.

Every one of those things is still true in 3026. The fire may be a fusion lamp in a habitat on the far side of the Sun, and the stories may travel for hours at the speed of light — but the shape of an evening is the same. People gather in a little light and tell each other what happened.

That is the deepest lesson of the mirror. A thousand years changed nearly everything humans could *do*. It changed almost nothing about what humans *want*.

If the people of 3026 are wiser than us, it is not because they are cleverer. It is because they had more time: more years in a life, more generations alive at once, more voices in the chorus, and more distance in which to be different.

## XIV. To the Reader in 3026
<!-- viz: dawn | year: 3026 | era: Epilogue | accent: #ffd9a0 -->

If you are reading this in 3026, then we were wrong about most of it. That is all right. A monk in 1026 would have been wrong about us too — and his guesses would still have been worth making.

We lived on the steep part of the curve. We were the first to see the Earth whole, a small bright world hanging in the dark. The first who could speak to the entire planet at once. The first who could break it. We hope we didn't.

We hope you can see the Milky Way from wherever you live. We hope your children know their great-great-grandparents. We hope you still argue, still sing, and still tell stories around some kind of fire.

And we hope that you, too, are looking a thousand years ahead — and that what you imagine is as impossible to you as you are to us.

— Written in 2026, a thousand years after the candle.

## Afterword: How This Was Imagined
<!-- viz: credits | year: 3026 | era: Notes -->

This story uses one playful rule: measure the ratio of change between 1026 and 2026 along a few dimensions of human life, then imagine that ratio repeating by 3026. Where a ratio cannot physically repeat — population on a single planet, the speed of a message — the story follows where the pressure would most likely go instead.

Figures for 1026 are estimates, and historians argue about them. World population around the year 1000 is usually put between 250 and 350 million. Pre-modern life expectancy at birth of roughly 25 to 35 years reflects very high child mortality rather than short adult lives. Energy use in agrarian societies is estimated from food, fodder and firewood. The 600× travel ratio compares a galloping horse, at about 60 km/h, with Apollo 10's re-entry speed of nearly 40,000 km/h.

The projections for 3026 are not forecasts. They are a thought experiment, and they deliberately err on the side of wonder, because every previous century that tried to imagine the next one erred the other way.
