# Max AI Vertical Ops Assistant

## Hero Thesis

Max AI should not start as “AI for everyone.” The practical wedge is an internal AI ops assistant for vertical service businesses: help the owner, dispatcher, or technician move faster first, then turn the same trusted context into the customer-facing agent later.

The long-term model can resemble Shopify plus Shop. Sell to businesses first. If the product works, customers eventually recognize and use Max AI across many businesses. That is B2B now, with a path to B2B2C later.

## Problem

Max AI has three plausible business models:

1. **B2C:** sell directly to consumers.
2. **B2B:** sell AI workflow software to businesses.
3. **B2B2C:** businesses pay, but customers also learn to use Max AI across many merchants or service providers.

The best long-term shape is B2B2C, but the mistake would be building the consumer network first. That path needs trust, integrations, consumer habit, and capital before there is proof that one segment wants the product.

The better starting problem is smaller and more operational. Vertical service businesses already live inside messy scheduling systems, job notes, calls, texts, parts questions, and follow-up reminders. Owners and technicians need quick answers:

- What is my next appointment?
- What happened with this customer?
- What parts should we order?
- Who needs a follow-up?
- What should I tell the customer now?

That is the first job. Not a broad agent platform. Not a polished consumer app. A narrow internal assistant that saves time in a real workday.

The home-service wedges are now clearer: **HVAC, plumbing, and electrical** should sit at the top of the first-pass priority list. They match the voice memo’s direction toward vertical service businesses and are close to the in-home service wedge discussed there.

The contrarian callout is **insurance agencies**. They should not be ignored. The repetitive communication load is obvious: proof of insurance, add a vehicle, certificate requests, billing questions, and routine customer updates. The category has strong margins and increasingly modern software stacks. It may be a high-margin expansion wedge, or even a parallel test, if the home-service pilots do not move quickly.

## Recommendation

Start with a vertical service wedge, not a generic Max AI launch.

Default priority:

1. **HVAC**
2. **Plumbing**
3. **Electrical**
4. **In-home appliance repair**, if the warm distribution path from the voice memo is real
5. **Insurance agencies**, as the contrarian high-margin test

The decision rule is Andy’s execution advice: **distribution beats idea quality**. Do not endlessly refine the concept. Pick the segment where warm access is strongest, launch fast, and let pilot usage tell the truth.

The product sequence should stay disciplined:

1. Internal AI ops assistant first.
2. Customer-facing agent second.
3. Cross-business Max AI identity only after the product is trusted inside the business.

## Product Concept

Max AI is the operating brain for a vertical service business.

The first product is an internal assistant for the owner, dispatcher, or technician. It connects to the systems the business already uses, such as scheduling, customer records, job notes, parts information, calls, texts, and follow-up history.

The assistant should answer operator-grade questions:

- “What is my next appointment?”
- “What do I need to know before this job?”
- “What parts should we order?”
- “What did this customer ask last time?”
- “Who needs a reminder or follow-up?”
- “Draft the status update for this customer.”

The customer-facing layer comes later. Once the internal assistant is trusted, the same context can power inbound customer responses for booking, status, reminders, availability, and basic job questions.

This preserves the big idea without overbuilding it. Max AI can become the customer layer across many businesses, but it earns that right by first becoming useful to the business.

## MVP

The MVP should be narrow enough to ship quickly and useful enough to test daily usage.

**Pilot shape**

- 3 to 5 warm businesses.
- One starting vertical.
- First 60 to 90 days free.
- Paid conversion only after value is proven.
- Possible first price anchor around $100 per month, subject to actual willingness to pay.

**Workflow scope**

Start with four internal jobs:

1. Next appointment lookup.
2. Parts-to-order support.
3. Job and customer context summary.
4. Follow-up reminders and draft messages.

**Product constraints**

- No broad marketplace.
- No consumer app first.
- No generic AI platform pitch.
- No customer-facing bot until internal users trust the assistant.
- No claim that one vertical proves all verticals.

## GTM

The GTM should be direct, warm, and fast.

1. Pick the vertical with the strongest warm path, not the most elegant market map.
2. Get 3 to 5 pilot businesses live.
3. Sell the business outcome: fewer dropped follow-ups, faster job context, clearer parts planning, better customer communication.
4. Keep the pilot free for 60 to 90 days so usage can form before price pressure.
5. Convert to a simple paid plan after repeated use, possibly around $100 per month to start.
6. Expand inside the same vertical before jumping categories.
7. Run a separate insurance-agency test if a warm path appears, because the communication volume and margins may justify a different wedge.

The sales motion should match Andy’s advice: talk less, do more. The first proof is not a deck. It is a business using the assistant during the workday.

## Risks/Falsifiers

**Distribution risk:** the warm path may not produce real pilot access.

**Falsifier:** 3 to 5 warm businesses will not try the product even when the first 60 to 90 days are free.

**Usage risk:** operators may like the demo but not change daily behavior.

**Falsifier:** technicians, dispatchers, or owners do not ask repeat questions after the first week.

**Integration risk:** the tools in the target vertical may be harder to connect than expected.

**Falsifier:** the MVP cannot answer next appointment, job context, or follow-up questions from available systems within the pilot window.

**Pricing risk:** $100 per month may be too low for the work required or too high for perceived value.

**Falsifier:** pilots use the product but will not convert after value is shown.

**Sequencing risk:** launching the customer-facing agent too early could create trust and accuracy problems.

**Falsifier:** internal users are not willing to rely on the assistant before customers see it.

**Vertical-transfer risk:** insurance agencies may look attractive but require a different workflow and sales motion than home services.

**Falsifier:** a small insurance-agency test shows the repetitive communication is real, but the buying motion or integration surface does not transfer.

## Next Steps

1. Choose the first vertical using warm distribution as the deciding factor.
2. Prioritize HVAC, plumbing, and electrical, with in-home appliance repair kept alive if the voice-memo distribution path is real.
3. Write a one-page pilot offer for 3 to 5 businesses: free for 60 to 90 days, internal assistant only, paid only after value is proven.
4. Define the first four workflows: next appointment, parts to order, job and customer context, follow-up reminders.
5. Map the required systems for the first pilot: schedule, customer records, job notes, parts data, calls, texts, and message history.
6. Build the smallest internal assistant that can answer useful daily questions from real business data.
7. Measure usage before satisfaction: repeat questions, daily use, saved follow-ups, and operator reliance.
8. Test the first paid conversion around $100 per month only after the pilot has real usage.
9. Keep insurance agencies on the test list as a contrarian high-margin wedge, especially if a warm intro appears.
10. Defer the customer-facing agent until internal trust is visible.
